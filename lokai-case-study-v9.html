01 — Problem

She got the notice three weeks ago. She still hasn't opened it.

Over 4.5 crore court cases are pending in India (National Judicial Data Grid, 2024). Most disputes never become formal filings at all. An estimated 5+ crore Indians live in states where the official legal language is not their mother tongue (Census 2011 migration data). Legal notices, RTI forms, and consumer complaints are issued in the local state language. For interstate residents, a notice is both a document they can't read and a process they can't navigate.

Priya, 31, moved from Lucknow to Andheri two years ago. Last month her landlord handed her a printed notice in Marathi. She can't read Marathi. She has no idea if it's routine paperwork, a rent demand, or something she needed to respond to a week ago. She asked her neighbour for a rough translation. She googled parts of it in Hindi. She asked her office colleague who "knows a lawyer." Nobody told her what to actually do. The notice sits on her kitchen counter. She's been afraid to touch it.

This is not a literacy problem. This is not even a language problem. This is an action problem.

The scale of inaction

Pending cases
5M+
Cases in consumer courts — not because people lack legal standing, but because most disputes never become formal filings.
Interstate residents
5cr+
Indians living in states where the official language is not their mother tongue (Census 2011). Legal notices are issued in the local state language.
Free legal aid
NALSA
Provides free legal aid to eligible citizens. Most have never heard of it. Access is not the same as awareness.

The gap between "I know something is wrong" and "I filed something" is where justice gets lost — not in courts, but in kitchens.

The real problem: For Indians living outside their home state, a legal notice in an unfamiliar language is both a linguistic barrier and a procedural one. For those who can read the language but not legal terminology, the barrier is a different kind. Both groups are large. Neither has a clear path from notice to action.
02 — Users (Before the Solution)

What do people actually do when they face a legal situation?

Before designing anything, I needed to understand two things: who encounters this problem, and what do they do today that fails them.

Arjun Krishnan
26 · Coimbatore · Delivery platform worker
Situation Account deactivated without explanation. Earning gone. Tamil is his primary language.

These are not edge cases. These are the majority of Indians who encounter legal friction every week — and do nothing, because the system that's supposed to protect them doesn't speak to them in a way they can act on.

03 — The Reframe

I spent two weeks designing a solution to the wrong problem.

LokAI started as a hackathon idea. The obvious framing: "People don't understand their legal rights because of language barriers." That's a comprehension problem. The natural solution is better translation, clearer summaries, a legal Wikipedia with multilingual support.

I started designing that. Then I noticed: every feature I was most drawn to was an action feature — document generators, complaint drafts, RTI applications. Not explainers. I stopped and asked a different question.

The question that broke the frame: What do people do when they already understand the problem but still don't act?

Arjun knows his account was deactivated unfairly. Priya knows the notice feels wrong. Neither lacks understanding. Both lack a pathway. The problem was never comprehension. It was the gap between knowing and doing.

What desk research confirmed

Consumer protection data Complaint filing rates, awareness workshop focus areas People rarely file despite having valid grievances

Competitive landscape NyayGuru, VakilAI, consumerhelpline.gov.in Market optimizes for professionals; government portals are form-heavy with no guidance layer

This is desk research, not fieldwork. A production product needs interviews at the exact moment someone gave up trying to file. That's where the real design problems live. Every assumption about the action step here is designed, not observed.

Comprehension is a prerequisite. It is not the outcome.

LokAI is not designed to make people understand the law. It is designed to get them to the counter.

Cases don't fail in court. They fail at the counter — because people never showed up.

04 — Product & Infrastructure

The tools existed. The experience didn't.

The research landscape scan surfaced four platforms that had arrived before LokAI — NyayGuru, JuniorLawyer, VakilAI, LawLens. All multilingual. All capable. All with developer-level UX.

What competitors do well
Multilingual legal information
Comprehensive legal database
Document templates available
Multiple Indian languages supported
What's missing
Designed for anxious users, not curious ones
Clarity over completeness
One clear next action, not a menu of options
Voice-first input for non-typing users

The gap is experience design, not features. These products were built to demonstrate legal capability. None were designed from the user's emotional state outward. NyayGuru asks users to select from 47 legal categories before stating their problem. That's not a UX flaw; it's a product designed for someone who already knows what type of legal issue they have. LokAI is for the person who doesn't know that yet, and who is already anxious. Different product, different user, significantly larger market.

Decision → Early drafts of the case study treated LokAI as "just another chatbot." I kept pushing back. The differentiator was never the feature set — it was designing for the emotional state of someone in a legal situation they don't fully understand. That framing changed everything.

LokAI is a guided decision system, not a chatbot

The distinction matters for design. A chatbot is open-ended — it responds to anything, generates conversation, treats the interface as a dialogue. A guided decision system has a clear job: take a situation, assess it, produce a clear position, and offer one next action.

Action-first, not information-first
Every response ends with something the user can do, not just understand
Clarity over completeness
Show what matters for this situation, not the entire legal landscape
Guided, not open-ended
Defined scope, clearly communicated when something falls outside it
Multilingual natively
Sarvam AI reasons in the language — Tamil, Bengali, Marathi — not via English translation

What this determines about the product

The scope is intentionally narrow in v1: RTI applications, consumer complaints, demand notices, and notice interpretation. These cover the majority of disputes an ordinary citizen can handle without legal representation. Both have established formats, clear filing procedures, and statutory response timelines that make the system reliable and specific.

Anything outside this scope — criminal law, court proceedings, contested property disputes — is explicitly declined, with specific human pathways offered instead. The boundary is not a limitation. It is the product's integrity.

Why not just use ChatGPT or Gemini directly?

Sarvam is the engine. LokAI is the vehicle designed for a specific road and a specific passenger.

General-purpose LLMs are excellent at exploring ideas. They are unreliable for jurisdiction-specific legal action.

Jurisdiction problem
They hallucinate across legal systems
A general LLM may cite UK consumer law in response to an Indian complaint. It has no mechanism to constrain itself to Indian law, RTI formats, or state-specific rules. The user cannot tell when this happens.
Output structure problem
They produce conversation, not decisions
ChatGPT returns open-ended text. LokAI implements a fixed flow: situation → assessment → legal position → ready-to-file document with citations. That structure is a product layer — not an LLM capability.
Emotional design problem
Not designed for anxious users
A generic chat interface puts the burden of framing and interpretation on the user. LokAI handles all three — in the user's language, with a curated verifiable source, with one clear next action.
LokAI will
Generate RTI applications, consumer complaints, demand notices
Interpret government notices in any Indian language
Route to human help when it cannot assist
LokAI will not
Handle criminal law, bail, or court proceedings
Replace a lawyer or provide legal advice
File documents on your behalf or guarantee outcomes

05 — Core Experience

One flow. Deeply designed.

The most important experience in LokAI is not the dashboard or the document generator. It is the moment a user describes their situation and receives a structured response that tells them exactly where they stand.

This flow — input → processing → assessment → action — is the product. Everything else supports it.

User input
Voice / text / photo — any Indian language
Language detection
Sarvam STT / OCR — native, not translated
Legal knowledge retrieval
Curated DB — IndiaCode, India Kanoon, CIC
Structured output
Structured response card + citation stamp + one action

The model retrieves from a curated, versioned database — never from the open internet. Every legal claim carries the source tag it was retrieved with. That's where citations come from: retrieved, not generated.

The response card — the product's most important artefact

Most AI products return responses as flowing text in a chat bubble. For legal information, this fails the user. A wall of text is unscannable. A chat bubble scrolls away. The most important sentence gets buried.

LokAI's response is a structured response card — three distinct zones, visually separated, with clear hierarchy:

Zone 1
What the law says
The relevant legal position, with a citation stamp confirming the source
Zone 2
Your position
A plain language assessment specific to the user's situation
Zone 3
What to do next
One primary action, not a menu of options
Why this works:
→ Zone 1 builds trust (law)
→ Zone 2 builds relevance (user)
→ Zone 3 drives action

The main legal finding is set in large DM Serif Display italic — large, confident, readable in seconds. Below it: the citation stamp. Together they communicate: this is a verified legal fact, and here is the proof.

The voice experience — trust in your own language

For many LokAI users, typing a complex legal situation is harder than speaking it. The voice input is not just a convenience feature — it is the primary interface for a significant portion of the user base.

When a user speaks, the screen shows live transcription as the words appear — word by word, in their language, with a blinking cursor. Below the transcript: a language detection chip that confirms "हिन्दी" or "தமிழ்" was understood. This is a high-trust signal. The moment a user sees their words appearing correctly in their language, they trust that the system understood them.

06 — Design Decisions

Every decision was a response to a specific user problem.

Hybrid conversational interface over pure chat Response design
Problem Legal information in a scrolling chat is unscannable, but a purely structured, form-based interface feels robotic and lacks empathy for an anxious user navigating a dispute.
Decision A deliberate modality shift. LokAI uses standard chat bubbles for intake, clarifying questions, and empathy. Once a legal conclusion is reached, the UI shifts to outputting a structured Assessment Card.
Rejected Standard open-ended chat UI. Rigid intake forms that strip the user of the ability to tell their story.
Naming note Early versions called the structured output a "VerdictCard." Renamed to Assessment Card — a verdict implies judicial finality that LokAI has no authority to deliver. The system assesses; it does not rule.
Tradeoff Requires clear visual distinction so the user understands when the system is just "talking" versus when it is delivering a definitive, cited legal finding.

Citation stamp as the only trust signal — no confidence percentages Trust system
Problem Users who need LokAI most have no reference point to evaluate whether a "72% confidence" badge means they should act or not. A percentage puts the burden of interpretation on someone who can't carry it.
Decision The citation stamp (navy background, white monospace, e.g. "RTI Act 2005 · s.7(1)") is the only trust signal. Citation present = retrievable and verified. No citation = general guidance, and the writing signals this ("in most cases..." vs "Under Section 7(1)..."). Binary behavior, not a percentage.
Rejected Confidence percentage badges ("94% confident"). High/medium/low labels. Color-coded accuracy indicators.
Turning point — Priya and the 94% badge

I put "94% confident" on a wireframe and tried to read it from Priya's position.

She has a landlord notice she doesn't fully understand. What does 94% mean to her? That LokAI is almost sure. She still has to decide whether "almost sure" is enough to act on.

The burden of interpreting that number is entirely on her.

What would actually help her act? Not a number — a source she can check herself. "RTI Act 2005 · s.7(1)" is something she can show a lawyer, a forum, or a friend who knows more.

A confidence score ends with LokAI. A citation opens outward. That's what an anxious user needs.

Tradeoff Harder to explain to stakeholders who want a visible accuracy metric. But the user acts on what the system does, not on a number they cannot interpret.

The citation stamp in context:

A landlord has no automatic right to withhold a security deposit. It must be returned within a reasonable period — typically 30 days. At 65 days, the delay is well past any reasonable standard. RTI Act 1999 · Section 24

Pinned citations via a "Sticky" Sidebar Cognitive load
Problem Legal disputes require context. If a user scrolls up through long chat history to remember the specific legal section cited earlier, they lose confidence and leverage.
Decision High-consequence legal facts and citation stamps are "pinned" to a right-hand sidebar. As the conversational dialogue continues to scroll on the left, the core legal position remains static and constantly visible on the right.
Also fixed Eliminates the memory tax. The user doesn't have to hold complex legal sections in their working memory while discussing their next steps. The leverage never scrolls out of sight.
Boundary moment as calm redirect, not error state Scope design
Problem When a user asks about something LokAI can't reliably handle (criminal law, court proceedings), a generic error or disclaimer destroys the trust built up to that point.
Decision "This needs a lawyer, not an AI." A specific response that names what it cannot do and immediately offers three concrete human pathways: NALSA free legal aid, Nyaaya helpline, and the paid Connect option. No red. No alarming iconography. The emotional register is calm and helpful.
Turning point — the wrong emotional register

The first version used a red error state — standard out-of-scope treatment, red border, disclaimer text.

I put it in front of a user who had described a situation involving a family member's arrest.

Red in a legal context says one thing: you are in trouble.

But the user isn't in trouble. LokAI just can't help with that specific thing. The product's boundary was being communicated as the user's failure.

The calm redirect came from that. No red. Plain prose. Three immediate alternatives. The message it sends: this is not your problem — it is our boundary.

Why it works A product that honestly says "I can't help with this, here's who can" builds more trust than one that tries and fails. The boundary is not a weakness — it is the product being responsible.

Multilingual identity through content and color, not symbols Identity
Problem Early logo used "लो" (Devanagari, first syllable of "Lok") — which made LokAI look like a Hindi-only app. Indian identity was achieved through decoration, not design.
Decision Logo uses a Latin "L" in a circle — universally readable, not language-specific. Indian identity is expressed through: language chips in native scripts (हिन्दी, தமிழ், বাংলা), Hindi chat conversations as demo content, Devanagari in the hero band. The product demonstrates multilingual capability; the logo does not have to carry it alone.
Turning point — "Is this just for Hindi?"

An early logo used "लो" in Devanagari. I showed it to someone. Their first question: "Is this just for Hindi?"

Two seconds. That question answered itself.

The logo was trying to signal "Indian" through one script from 22 official languages. Every Tamil, Bengali, or Marathi speaker would ask the same thing.

The insight wasn't that the logo needed to be neutral. It was that multilingual identity was being claimed by a symbol instead of demonstrated by the product.

Language chips in native scripts, Hindi as the demo conversation, Devanagari in the hero band — that's evidence. "लो" was a claim.

Rejected Ashoka Chakra loading animations. Jaali-work overlays. "Indian craftsmanship" gavel icons. All decoration over function.

07 — Key Action Features

Moving from understanding to execution.

The structured response card gives the user clarity, but the product only succeeds if they can actually act on that clarity. LokAI shifts the burden of execution off the user through two core tools.

Notice Interpreter — OCR + LLM Intake
Problem A migrant worker receiving a municipal notice in Kannada cannot type it out to ask a chatbot what it means.
Solution The user snaps a photo. Sarvam's OCR extracts the native script, and the model interprets the legal intent. The UI presents a split-screen: the original document on the left (to build trust that nothing was missed) and the plain-language assessment on the right.
Hierarchy If the notice contains a legal deadline, that date becomes the largest, saffron-bordered element on the screen. The most prominent UI element carries the highest consequence for inaction.

Document Generator Output
Problem Telling a user "You should file an RTI" is useless if they don't know the statutory format required for the filing to be accepted.
Solution LokAI generates the formal document based on the chat context. The UI clearly separates AI boilerplate from user-specific details that must be reviewed.
Visual rule Every editable field — names, dates, amounts — uses a 2px dashed saffron underline. This prevents users from blindly submitting hallucinated placeholders and forces a review of the variables that matter.

08 — Edge Cases & Failure States

A legal product earns trust at the edges, not just the happy path.

Every failure state runs on the same principle: do not alarm the user unnecessarily. This person is already anxious. When something breaks, the interface stays calm.

Graceful degradation.

Denying permissions should not block the user. A primary CTA offers a direct fix via Settings, while the UI instantly pivots to a text-first fallback.

Network / model error

Two recovery actions.

Try again + Copy my message. The second matters — the user typed out a stressful situation and should not lose it to a network failure.

09 — Projected Business Impact

What it means when someone actually files.

The calculation

Government digital services without guided assistance see low completion rates for multi-step legal processes. DARPG's NeSDA 4.0 assessment (2023) documents government portal service delivery performance across India; multi-step legal services consistently fall below 25% completion without assisted guidance. LokAI's structured Response Card, with native-language output and a single clear next action, targets a meaningful improvement over that baseline. Applying a conservative 15-point uplift to 10,000 monthly queries: approximately 1,500 additional formal complaints or RTI applications drafted per month that wouldn't have been otherwise.

At district scale, that reduces the informal help-desk query load that currently absorbs clerk hours without producing any formal record. The design benefit isn't just user experience. It's the formal complaint system working more like it should.

Methodology: Formula 05 (estimation with stated methodology). Baseline from DARPG NeSDA 4.0, 2023. Uplift estimate based on published research on language-aligned guided flows in government services. These are projections, not post-launch data.

Three metrics that matter

Action completion rate
Primary
Did the user download or share a generated document? Not just read the response, but produce something they could file. Target: above 40% of sessions that reach the response card.
Boundary honesty rate
Safety
What percentage of out-of-scope queries route to human help rather than generating a low-confidence document? A product that confidently drafts a bail application is actively harmful. This tells you whether the scope boundary is working, not just present in the UI.
Language input distribution
Product health
Split of voice / Romanized text / native script across sessions. If voice is below 30%, there's a discovery or trust problem. If native script is near zero, users are defaulting to English anyway, and the multilingual infrastructure isn't being used.

What's next

A/B test the citation stamp format. Hypothesis: showing a specific statutory reference ("Consumer Protection Act, 2019 §35") increases form submission rate more than a general reference ("Indian consumer protection law"). If action completion doesn't improve with specific citations, the barrier isn't trust in the information. It's the filing process itself, and the next intervention shifts to form pre-fill integration with government portals.

The hallucination risk is mitigated by the citation architecture, not solved by it. A production LokAI needs periodic legal audits: human lawyers reviewing outputs against actual legal outcomes. The design acknowledges this layer. It does not pretend to solve it.

LokAI doesn't win when it answers correctly. It wins when someone files something they never thought they could.
