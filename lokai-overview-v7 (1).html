Designing AI that knows its place in Indian law.

A multilingual guided decision system that takes an Indian citizen from a legal situation they don't understand to a document they can actually file — in their own language, in a single session.

What it is
A mobile app for Indian citizens facing a legal situation they don't know how to act on. You describe your situation — in voice, text, or photo, in any Indian language — and LokAI tells you your legal position, names the exact law that applies, and produces a document you can file. Not a chatbot. Not a legal encyclopedia. A pathway from "I received this notice and I don't know what it means" to "I have something I can actually do."
Who it's for
A migrant worker in Maharashtra gets an eviction notice in Marathi. She can't read it. The system built to help her — consumer courts, RTI portals, NALSA helplines — assumes she already knows what the notice means and what to do next. That assumption fails 800 million people. LokAI is designed for that first step.
Core idea
Understanding was never the barrier. The barrier is the first step. Every response ends in something the user can do. Success is not whether they read the response — it's whether they filed.

Research Direction

Four competitors already existed. The differentiator had to be experience, not features.

NyayGuru, JuniorLawyer, VakilAI, LawLens — all had reasonable multilingual coverage. The gap in all four was the same: built by developers to demonstrate capability, not designed from the user's emotional state outward.

Before
Build a better legal Wikipedia: multilingual, comprehensive, well-structured.
Finding
I started with a comprehension problem. Two weeks in, every feature I was drawn to was an action feature: document generators, not explainers. The problem was never reading. It was filing.
After
Every response must end in something the user can do. The product's measure of success is not whether they understood — but whether they filed.

One user interview at the point of abandonment would have surfaced this in an afternoon. All research here was desk research. That gap is honest about its consequences: every assumption about the action step is designed, not observed.

Key Design Decisions

Three decisions that shaped the product itself, not just the interface.

01
Hybrid interface — conversational intake, Assessment Card output. Three-zone structured response.
Single-mode chat thread throughout, with "VerdictCard" output. Legal analysis, verdict, next steps — structured like a judge's summary. → Two modes: chat bubbles for intake and clarification. Once a legal conclusion is reached, the UI shifts to a structured Assessment Card — THE LAW / YOUR POSITION / WHAT YOU CAN DO — surfaced inline in the thread. The name change from VerdictCard is also part of this decision.
Legal information in a scrolling chat is unscannable. A purely structured form feels robotic to someone already anxious. The deliberate mode shift is the product. "VerdictCard" implies judicial finality LokAI has no authority to deliver — the name is where the trust boundary is set, before the user reads a word of the output.

02
Assessment Card
Screen — Assessment Card
The chat thread shows the user's intake and LokAI's clarifying questions. The Assessment Card surfaces inline once a legal conclusion is reached — not on a separate page. The modality shift happens in context, not behind a navigation event.
02
Citation stamp + pinned sidebar — verifiable trust that stays in view
Confidence badge ("87% confident") — a single chat thread where cited sections scroll off-screen as conversation continues → Citation stamp in navy ("RTI Act 2005 · s.7(1)") is the only trust signal. High-consequence facts pin to a sticky sidebar — static while the conversation scrolls on the left.
A confidence score ends with LokAI. A citation opens outward — the user can show it to a lawyer, a friend, a forum. Pinning it to a sidebar eliminates the memory tax: an anxious user should not have to scroll back through a conversation to find the section that applies to them.
05
Citation sidebar
Screen — Citation sidebar
The right column is static. Chat scrolls left; pinned legal position stays right. The user can continue asking follow-up questions without losing the core answer. Each citation chip is tappable — it opens the source statute directly.
03
Defined scope over open-ended capability
An open chat covering all legal queries — maximally capable, but unreliable. General-purpose AI hallucinates across legal jurisdictions. → RTI, consumer complaints, demand notices, and notice interpretation only. Everything else explicitly declined — with specific human pathways (NALSA, Nyaaya helpline, Connect).
Reliability within a defined domain builds more trust than best-effort across an unbounded one. When something falls outside scope, the response is a calm redirect: "This needs a lawyer, not an AI." No red error state. The boundary is the product's integrity — and it's the only thing preventing LokAI from drafting a bail application for someone facing criminal charges.
10
Out-of-scope redirect
Screen — Scope boundary
When a query falls outside scope, LokAI names the reason calmly and provides a specific human pathway — NALSA, Nyaaya, or a Connect referral. No red state. No alarm. The user leaves with a next step, not a dead end.
Edge states & failure behaviour
Microphone denied
Voice orb turns grey with a lock icon. Input shifts to native-script text. Full functionality retained — voice is preferred, not required. No error modal.
Network / model error
Two recovery actions surface: Try again and Copy my message. The second matters most — the user typed out a stressful legal situation and should not lose it to a network failure.
OCR fail — notice upload
Low-resolution photo returns a re-upload prompt with framing tips. Conversation context is preserved — no session reset, no cold start. The user does not begin again.
Out of scope
Calm redirect with a named human pathway. No red state, no alarm. The boundary is stated plainly — "This needs a lawyer, not an AI."
Why Not a General-Purpose LLM

Three failure modes that rule out ChatGPT, Gemini, or any open-ended model.

This was an explicit design constraint, not a positioning choice. A general-purpose LLM fails in a legal access product in ways that are not just inconvenient — they are harmful.

01
No scope enforcement — confident in the wrong domain
Ask ChatGPT to draft a bail application. It will. Confidently. A general-purpose model has no mechanism for "this is outside what I can reliably handle." For a product in legal contexts, that is not a UX problem — it is a harm problem. LokAI's scope boundary is enforced at the model behaviour level, not just shown as a UI message.
02
Citations are hallucinated — there is no retrieval
Ask GPT-4 about RTI Section 7(1) and it will give you a plausible-sounding answer. The statute reference may be wrong. The sub-section may not exist. LokAI uses retrieval-augmented generation against actual statute text — the citation is real, it is tappable, and the user can verify it independently.
03
Indian languages go through English — nuance gets compressed
Most multilingual LLMs translate Indian languages into English, reason in English, then translate back. Legal nuance — the specific wording of a Section, the difference between "shall" and "may" — is compressed or lost in that round-trip. Sarvam AI processes Tamil, Bengali, Marathi, and Hindi natively. No English intermediary.

What I'd Measure

Three metrics, each answering a real question about whether this worked.

Action completion rate
Primary
Did the user download or share a generated document? Not whether they read the response — whether they produced something fileable. Target: above 40% of sessions that reach the Assessment Card.
Boundary honesty rate
Safety
What percentage of out-of-scope queries route to human help rather than generating a low-confidence document? A product that confidently drafts a bail application is actively harmful.
Language input distribution
Product health
Split of voice, Romanized text, and native script across sessions. If voice is below 30%, there's a discovery or trust problem with the input design. If native script is near zero, the no-keyboard-setup assumption held.
Honest Reflection

What I'd change, what I got right, and what the product still doesn't solve.

A UX decision I'd revisit
The dashboard shows Chat Summaries: retrospective cards of what was discussed. A user returning to an ongoing legal dispute doesn't want a digest. They want to continue exactly where they stopped. The summaries should be continuation prompts, not retrospectives. I designed for the wrong returning-user mental model.
What the product still doesn't solve
The hallucination risk is mitigated by the citation architecture: source-tagged retrieval reduces unverifiable claims. But it is not eliminated. A production LokAI would need periodic legal audits by human lawyers reviewing outputs against actual case outcomes. The design acknowledges this layer. It does not pretend to solve it.
LokAI doesn't win when it answers correctly. It wins when someone files something they never thought they could.
